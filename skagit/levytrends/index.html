A Message From Your Assessor

Property taxes pay for our schools, roads, fire protection, and much more, but how they are set can feel like a mystery. I built this tool so anyone in Skagit County can see where the money goes and why it changes, using the same certified numbers my office works from.

The numbers show what changed and why. They are not a report card on any district. Whether a levy is money well spent is for you, your neighbors, and the people you elect to decide. My hope is that this helps all of us ask better questions.

This tool is new, and we built it from scratch. If something looks wrong or confusing, please tell me at dhagen@skagitcountywa.gov. I read every message.

Danny Hagen

Start With a Question

How to Use This Tool
  1. Whole county or one district. Whole county shows how all the property tax levied in Skagit County in a year is divided among schools, cities, the county, and every other district, and what that comes to for each county resident. One district at a time shows a single district’s full history.
  2. Pick what to measure at the top. Levy Rate is the price per $1,000 of value. Levied Amount is the total dollars a district levies for the year. Assessed Value is the tax base itself.
  3. Pick a category, then check one or more districts. Select all compares a whole category at once. You can check and uncheck freely.
  4. Hover or tap the chart to see any year in detail, including how much it changed from the year before and from five years back. Diamonds mark years where something notable happened; select one for the explanation and the source. With a keyboard, move to the chart and use the left and right arrow keys to step through the years, then press Enter on a diamond year.
  5. On Levied Amount you can compare a district against inflation, measured two ways, against population growth, or against both together, or switch every year into 2026 dollars.
  6. Choose where “since” starts. Every comparison is measured from a starting year, 2010 unless you change it. A different starting year can change the picture, so try more than one.
  7. Three ways to read the whole county table. Above the Every Year at a Glance table, choose Dollars for the amounts as levied, Share of total for each group’s slice of the county total, or Per resident for dollars per county resident with earlier years restated in today’s dollars.
  8. Below the chart: the district’s story in plain language, a year by year timeline of what changed, and the full data table. Where a large change has no explanation yet, the timeline says Not yet explained instead of guessing.
  9. Check our numbers. In any table, select a year at the top of a column to open that year’s certified rate sheet, the official record these figures come from.
  10. Share what you see. The Copy link to this view button above the chart copies a link that opens this tool on the exact districts, measure, and comparisons you have selected.
What These Terms Mean
Taxing district
A government service area with authority to levy property tax: the county, a city, a school district, a fire district, and so on. Most homes sit inside eight to ten overlapping districts, and a tax bill is the sum of all of them.
Levied amount
The total dollars a district bills to all property owners for the year, as certified by the Assessor. What is actually paid in can differ a little because of late payments and refunds.
Levy rate
The price per $1,000 of assessed value. A rate of $1.00 on a $400,000 home is $400 for the year. The rate is how a district’s total gets divided among properties.
Assessed value
The county’s estimate of what a property would sell for, updated every year.
The 1 percent limit
A district’s regular levy in dollars can grow only about 1 percent per year, plus what new construction adds, unless voters approve more (RCW 84.55.010). Rising values do not raise a district’s total. When values rise faster than 1 percent, rates fall.
New construction
New buildings add to a district’s total outside the 1 percent limit, so a district’s levied amount can grow without raising anyone’s existing bill.
Major and junior districts
Majors are the state school levy, local school districts, cities and towns, and county funds. Juniors are the smaller special purpose districts: fire, hospital, library, parks, cemetery, and port.
Special assessments
Flat charges for dike, drainage, and similar districts. They are set in dollars, not as a rate per $1,000, so they appear only under Levied Amount.
Lid lift
A voter approved increase that lets a district levy more than the 1 percent limit would allow, up to a legal maximum rate (RCW 84.55.050). Many run six years with a built in annual escalator. A lift described as permanent means the higher amount becomes the new base once the lift period ends, so the levy does not drop back.
Banked capacity
When a district levies less than the maximum allowed, it can save the difference and levy it in a later year without a new vote (RCW 84.55.092).
Bond levy
Voter approved debt for buildings and equipment, paid through a separate levy until the bond retires. Refinancing an existing bond at lower cost does not require a vote.
EMS levy
A voter approved levy for emergency medical services, often attached to a fire district (RCW 84.52.069).
State school levy and McCleary
The statewide school levy is set by the Legislature, not the county. After the Supreme Court’s McCleary decision it was restructured in 2018 into two parts. The State School Total in this tool shows the combined, continuous series so years before and after compare directly.
Equalization
Counties do not all assess at exactly the same level, so the state evens things out. Each year the Department of Revenue compares assessed values with actual sale prices and sets a ratio for every county (RCW 84.48.075). The state school levy is then divided among the counties using those equalized values, so each county pays its fair share (RCW 84.48.080). That is why the state school rate shown here is not exactly the statewide rate the Legislature set, such as $2.70 in 2018. The same idea applies to a district that crosses a county line: its levy is shared between the counties on an equalized basis, so the Skagit rate times the Skagit value will not exactly match the Skagit amount.
Share and points
A group’s share is its slice of all the property tax levied in the county that year. Changes in share are given in percentage points: a share that goes from 20 percent to 25 percent is up 5 points. That is different from the levy itself rising 5 percent. A group’s share can fall even while its dollars rise, if other groups grew faster.
Not yet explained
A year where a district’s levied amount moved by more than 8 percent and we have not yet confirmed why. We would rather say so than guess. If you know the story, tell us.
Diamonds on the chart
A diamond marks a year where something notable happened to that district. Select it for the explanation and the source.
2026 dollars
A toggle that adjusts past years for Seattle area inflation so amounts compare in today’s purchasing power. On the Levied Amount view you can also show both at once: the amount as actually levied in each year’s dollars, and the same amount restated in 2026 dollars. The two lines meet in 2026, and the gap between them is what inflation accounts for.
Inflation and population pace lines
Optional dashed lines showing where a levy would be if it had grown only with inflation, only with population, or both together. Inflation plus population is the benchmark most often proposed as an alternative to the 1 percent limit.
Two ways to measure inflation
The Seattle area Consumer Price Index (CPI) tracks what households here pay, and it is what the 2026 dollars view uses. The implicit price deflator (IPD) is a national measure, and it is the one state levy law itself uses to define inflation (RCW 84.55.005). Seattle prices have risen faster than the national measure, so the two lines can sit well apart. Neither one measures what it costs to run a fire district or a school.
Starting year
Every “since” comparison and every pace line begins at a starting year you can change. The default, 2010, falls in the recession, when some districts were at unusually low or capped levels. Moving the starting year shows whether a conclusion holds or depends on where you begin.
Per resident, after inflation
The whole county view divides each line by the county’s population and restates earlier years in today’s dollars. It answers a plain question: is each resident, on average, being asked for more than before once prices and growth are accounted for? It is an average across the county, not any one household’s bill.
Why levies can outgrow the benchmarks
The pace lines are context, not a verdict. Districts face real cost drivers beyond inflation and headcount: service model changes such as Sedro-Woolley’s shift from volunteer to full-time paid firefighters, rising call volumes, state and federal mandates, and insurance and construction costs that outpace the general CPI. A levy above the benchmark lines is not automatically waste, and one below them is not automatically virtue. The year by year explanations on each district tell the specific story.

    About this tool. This is a public transparency tool published by the Office of the Skagit County Assessor, Danny Hagen, to help Skagit County residents see how their local property taxes are set and why they change. It is a new kind of tool, built from scratch by our office with no existing model to start from, and we expect to keep improving it.

    Where the numbers come from. Figures are drawn from the Assessor’s annual certified Property Tax Assessments and Levies rate sheets, tax years 2010 to 2026. Open any year’s sheet: . The same sheets are listed on the Assessor’s Levy Rates and Reports page (opens in a new tab). Levy rates are dollars per $1,000 of assessed value. “Levied Amount” is the total tax certified for the district that year, which is what the district bills and not what has been paid in. School district figures combine all levy components. State School is a single levy through 2017 and split into Part I and Part II from 2018. Districts that cross the county line show their Skagit County portion only.

    Explanations. Each year-by-year explanation names its source: the Assessor’s certified rate sheets or levy record, a ballot measure record, a statute or bill, or documented research. Where a large change has no explanation recorded yet, the tool says so.

    Inflation and population. Inflation comparisons use the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue, from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. Each year’s figure is the average of the published bimonthly index values; October 2025 was not published, so 2025 averages the five published values, and 2026 uses values through April 2026. The second inflation measure is the implicit price deflator (IPD) for personal consumption, the figure state levy law uses to define inflation (RCW 84.55.005 (opens in a new tab)), as the Washington Department of Revenue publishes it each September for taxes due the following year. Population comparisons use the OFM April 1 official population estimates for Skagit County and its cities and towns (intercensal series for 2010 to 2020, anchored to both census counts; postcensal estimates for 2021 to 2026). County-level lines use the countywide population, except County Road, which uses the unincorporated-area population it serves. Each tax year is paired with the April 1 population of the same year. In the whole county view, per resident figures divide every line by the countywide population so the lines add up to the total.

    The whole county view adds up every district’s certified levy for the year, including special assessments. General Fund Total and State School Part I and Part II are left out of that sum because their amounts are already counted in other lines.

    Corrections. This tool is provided for public information. For official certified figures, appeals, or questions about your own assessment, contact the Skagit County Assessor’s Office.
